CAPSTONE · SUMMATIVE

Build a Module Development Package

Take a module objective, decide how it gets met, and build one lesson of it for real.

Across the four units you planned, designed, built, and reviewed learning. This capstone puts all of it to work at once. You start from a module objective Design has given you, decide how it gets met, and build one lesson of it the way you would on the job, filed in a repo you create.

There is no grade and nothing goes to a gradebook. Your manager reviews the package against the standards you have been working to, gives you feedback, and makes one call: is this ready to build, or does it need another pass? A ready verdict is what endorses you to take on module development work.

How long this takes

Plan on 16 hours, which is two full working days. Do not try to do it in one sitting. Most people spread it over a week or two, and that is the right way to work on it.

A rough sense of where the sixteen hours go: about five on the plan, the blueprint and the AI-use record, four on the built lesson, three on the activity, two on the assessment and its rubric, and two on the facilitator-guide section. If you are four hours in and think you are nearly done, something in the package is thinner than it looks.

What you submit

A link to a Google Drive folder (your module repo), named to the convention and containing all eight items below, filed correctly. Submit the shared link, not individual files. The folder itself is the deliverable, so how you name and organize it counts.

Part 1: The plan (three documents)

This is the thinking, written down, so a reviewer can follow your decisions without you in the room.

1. The module plan
  • The module objective you were given. Quote it as it arrived from Design, and name the job task it builds toward. You are not rewriting it.
  • Your lesson objectives. The steps that break that module objective into teachable pieces. These are yours to write, and each one must be observable, measurable, and one behavior.
  • Your approach. The activities, the technology, and the sequence you chose, and why. Say what you ruled out as well as what you picked.
  • Your assessment plan. Every lab, check, and module-end summative, each named to the convention (for example GLAB 301.1.1, SBA 301) and mapped to the objective it measures. No orphan assessments, no unmeasured objectives.
  • The summative competency check. How the module ends with the learner solving a real problem unaided.
  • Your accessibility plan. What you will do as you build, rather than what you would fix afterward.
2. The module blueprint (MBP)

Every item in the module listed in delivery order, named to convention, with the built files linked. Use the Module Blueprint Template. This is how QA and Delivery navigate a build, so a blueprint that does not match the repo is a finding.

3. Your AI-use record

Half a page. What you drafted with an AI assistant, what you verified and how, what you corrected or cut, and what you wrote yourself. Using AI heavily is fine and expected. An unexamined draft is not. This is the same standard learners are held to, so we hold it visibly.

Module numbers like 301 are examples of the convention. Use the pattern, not these exact numbers.

Part 2: The built slice (five files)

This is the part that shows you can execute. Pick one lesson from your planned module and build it for real, along with everything that lesson needs in order to run.

4. The lesson

The actual teaching content for one lesson day, following the lesson-day rhythm: warm-up in, hands-on practice in the middle, exit ticket out. Time skewed toward the learner doing rather than the instructor talking.

5. One activity

A guided lab or an assignment lab giving real practice toward that lesson objective, for example GLAB 301.1.1 or ALAB 301.1.1. Every step: one action, named specifics, and a result the learner can see.

6. One aligned assessment

A check that measures that same lesson objective, with a type that fits it. A performance objective needs a performance task, not a quiz. Even a small assessment proves the rule that matters most: learners demonstrate mastery rather than only consume content.

7. A rubric for the graded item

Every graded item carries a rubric. Criteria from the objectives rather than from the steps of the activity, levels a reader can tell apart without looking at the points, and a total that reconciles with the gradebook. If the task has an AI component, the three AI Integrity Rubric criteria go in alongside yours.

8. The facilitator-guide section for that lesson

Written for an instructor who was in none of your design conversations and teaches this next month. Purpose per activity, timing and setup, where learners predictably get stuck, look-fors on the assessment, and what to do when the room is ahead, behind, or the environment fails. If your lesson needs you in the building, it is not finished.

One lesson, one activity, one assessment. A real slice built to standard, with everything that slice needs in order to run. Not a whole module.

The repo: how to file it

Create a Google Drive folder named to the module convention, then file every item using correct names and the right subfolders, exactly as you practiced in Unit 1. A reviewer should be able to find any file from its name alone.

📁 Module 301: [Your Module Name]
📁 Resources/
MBP 301  (the blueprint)
Module plan 301
FG 301.1  (facilitator guide for the built lesson)
AI-use record
📁 Lessons/
Lesson 301.1
📁 Assignments/
GLAB 301.1.1  (or ALAB 301.1.1)
📁 Assessments/
the aligned check for Lesson 301.1
its rubric

Structure and numbers shown are an example of the convention. Match the pattern to your own module.

How to submit
  1. Set the folder's share setting so your reviewer can open it, following your team's Drive sharing norms.
  2. Copy the folder's shared link.
  3. Paste the link into this assignment and submit.
  4. Review the package live with your manager or mentor. That conversation is the real capstone.
